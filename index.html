Women in Mexico have a rich history marked by deep cultural roots, pivotal roles in social change, and ongoing struggles for equality. Traditionally, the concept of machismo (male dominance) and its counterpart hembrismo (female submission) shaped rigid social expectations, placing women primarily at the heart of the family as devoted caregivers. Despite these traditional boundaries, Mexican women have historically acted as foundational pillars of their households and communities, blending warmth, resilience, and hard work into daily life.

During the Mexican Revolution of 1910, women stepped far beyond the domestic sphere, most famously as soldaderas who cooked, nursed, and sometimes fought on the front lines. This period sparked a long-term movement for civic participation, leading to milestones like the first Feminist Congresses in Yucatán and, eventually, the achievement of national voting rights in 1953. Cultural and intellectual icons such as the 17th-century scholar Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz and painter Frida Kahlo further demonstrated the profound intellectual and artistic impact of Mexican women through the centuries.

In contemporary society, Mexican women drive economic and political progress while confronting persistent systemic hurdles. Women participate heavily in the modern workforce and recently achieved a historic political milestone with the election of Claudia Sheinbaum as the country's first female president in 2024. At the same time, severe issues like gender-based violence, high rates of femicide, and economic informality remain critical challenges that spark widespread, ongoing activist movements across the nation.
